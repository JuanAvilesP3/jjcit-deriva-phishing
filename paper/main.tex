{\fontsize{13pt}{11pt} \textit{\textbf{ABSTRACT}}}\par
\setlength{\parskip}{6.0pt}
\fontsize{10pt}{12.0pt}\selectfont 
{\textit{Phishing detectors are almost universally evaluated with random train/test splits, which implicitly assume that a URL's characteristics are exchangeable across time. We test that assumption by training three lexical-feature classifiers (random forest, XGBoost, logistic regression) on 180 days of PhishTank-reported phishing URLs plus a matched sample of legitimate domains, and evaluating them under four temporal designs: a random split (the field's default), training on the earliest block and testing on each subsequent block, a sliding retraining window, and cumulative retraining. All three models achieve F1 above 0.98 in every configuration, and the gap between the random-split baseline and the worst temporally-displaced block is small in absolute terms (0.36 to 0.92 percentage points of F1, model-dependent). A regression of F1 on temporal distance is significant when pooled across models, and its precision improves substantially once model-level baseline differences are controlled for ($p=0.022$ naive, $p=0.0048$ controlling for model as a fixed effect); a Page test for a monotonic decreasing trend across the three models as judges agrees ($Z=-2.66$, $p=0.004$). We argue the near-ceiling performance points partly to a design choice rather than a solved problem: removing the four features most associated with how the legitimate class was constructed (\texttt{is\_https}, \texttt{path\_length}, \texttt{num\_slashes}, \texttt{url\_length}) drops F1 by 3.3 to 4.1 percentage points across models, confirming that the choice of negative class (well-established top-ranked domains) inflates the reported ceiling; the four ablated features are not the sole source of the near-ceiling result, though the remaining features may still encode the same domain-versus-full-URL asymmetry; a negative class of full legitimate URLs would be needed to settle this.}}\par

\section{Introduction}

Phishing is an adversarial instance of the broader concept-drift problem in machine learning~\citep{gama2014survey}: attackers change URL patterns, hosting infrastructure, and lexical tricks specifically to evade whatever a detector has already learned to flag. A classifier trained and evaluated with a single random train/test split treats every URL in the dataset as drawn from one stationary distribution, which is precisely the assumption an adversarial, evolving threat violates. If that assumption is wrong, published accuracy figures based on random splits systematically overstate what a detector will achieve once deployed against future, unseen phishing campaigns.

This paper asks how much that gap actually is, using a temporal evaluation design rather than a random one, and compares two common mitigation strategies, periodic retraining with a sliding window versus cumulative retraining that keeps all historical examples, to see whether either meaningfully closes it. We evaluate on 180 days of continuously reported phishing URLs, which lets us construct genuinely time-ordered evaluation blocks rather than simulating drift artificially.

The contributions of this work are: (1) a temporal, block-based evaluation of lexical phishing classifiers on real, timestamped data, contrasted directly against the random-split baseline the field defaults to; (2) a statistically defensible estimate of the degradation trend's significance, using an ANCOVA specification that separates within-model drift from between-model baseline differences, alongside a specification-free Page test as a cross-check; (3) a mechanistic account, grounded in feature-distribution and feature-importance evidence rather than aggregate metrics alone, of why the measured degradation is small: an easy negative class, not a solved drift problem.

\section{Related Work}

Odeh et al.~\citep{odeh2021phiboost} propose PhiBoost, an adaptive-boosting phishing detector built on lexical and host-based URL features, and report strong within-distribution accuracy using a conventional held-out test split, exactly the evaluation protocol this paper argues is insufficient on its own, since it cannot distinguish a detector that has learned durable phishing indicators from one that has overfit to the particular campaigns present in the training window. Sahingoz et al.~\citep{sahingoz2019phishing} benchmark seven classifiers (including Random Forest, the best-performing model in both their study and ours) on a mixed lexical-and-content feature set, and likewise report F1 scores above 0.97 using a standard random 80/20 split; their feature set and evaluation setup are directly comparable to ours, making the temporal gap we identify a direct reinterpretation of their baseline rather than a critique of the specific classifier choice. Alslman et al.~\citep{alslman2024ddos} evaluate ensemble models (the same broad model family we use here) for DDoS attack detection at scale using Spark, and, like most applied network-security classification work, do not examine whether detection performance is stable as the underlying attack traffic evolves over time; our temporal-block design is directly applicable to that setting as well, and we frame our contribution partly as a methodological recommendation that extends beyond phishing specifically to security classification tasks built on adversarial, time-varying data. Furthermore, in a systematic survey within this journal's scope, Ashi et al.~\citep{ashi2021nids} examine supervised machine-learning classifiers and dimensionality reduction techniques for network intrusion detection, underscoring that benchmarked detection performance is critically sensitive to feature choice and evaluation rigor; our study directly extends that methodological perspective by investigating how temporal drift affects lexical feature stability under longitudinal deployment.

This concern is not new to security classification in general: Pendlebury et al.~\citep{pendlebury2019tesseract} name and formalize the same failure mode for malware classifiers under the label \emph{temporal bias}, showing that a naive random train/test split can silently mix future and past samples and thereby overstate real-world performance, and propose constraints (training strictly on past data, realistic class-ratio testing) that our fixed, sliding-window, and cumulative designs already satisfy by construction; we are not aware of a comparable temporal-bias analysis having been applied to phishing URL classification specifically, which is the gap this paper addresses. Jemili et al.~\citep{jemili2024ids} extend this line of work to network intrusion detection, showing that a classifier that combines online concept-drift detection with incremental retraining substantially narrows the performance gap between static and adaptive systems; their empirical design confirms that drift is real and measurable in security-relevant classification streams, but they do not study lexical URL features or control for the contribution of class-boundary difficulty to the observed gap, which is the mechanistic question we pursue here. Apruzzese et al.~\citep{apruzzese2023real} survey the gap between adversarial machine-learning research and operational practice, arguing that real attackers rarely employ the gradient-based perturbations most studied in the literature, and that the threat models implicit in random-split evaluations are therefore doubly unrealistic: both the time ordering and the assumed adversarial capability depart from deployment conditions. We take that observation as additional motivation for a design that at least corrects the temporal dimension, even if it does not resolve the adversarial-realism dimension.

The challenge of concept drift in cyber security classification is documented at the survey level by Buczak and Guven~\citep{buczak2016survey}, who review over 50 machine-learning methods for intrusion detection and identify temporal non-stationarity as one of five core challenges for production deployment, alongside class imbalance, feature selection, computational cost, and the scarcity of labeled attack data. Critically, they find that no single study among those reviewed addresses all five simultaneously, and that temporal drift in particular is usually handled implicitly (by retraining from scratch on new data) rather than by a principled comparison of retraining strategies. Our study contributes precisely that comparison for the phishing-URL domain, testing fixed, sliding-window, and cumulative retraining under controlled temporal separation. Varshney, Misra and Atrey~\citep{varshney2016phishing} provide a complementary survey-level taxonomy, classifying phishing-detection schemes by feature type (visual, lexical, content-based, network-based); our study operates exclusively within their ``lexical'' category and is designed to answer a question their survey identifies but does not resolve empirically: whether lexical-only detectors maintain their reported accuracy as the phishing campaign population evolves over time.

The feature-engineering and modelling approaches evaluated in this paper have direct precedents in the phishing-detection literature. Mohammad, Thabtah and McCluskey~\citep{mohammad2014phishing} introduce an intelligent rule-based classifier for phishing websites using URL and page-level features, and in a companion study~\citep{mohammad2013neural} apply self-structuring neural networks to the same feature family; both report high accuracy under conventional random splits, making their baseline directly comparable to the random-split condition in our temporal design and therefore subject to the same temporal-bias concern that Pendlebury et al.~\citep{pendlebury2019tesseract} formalize for malware and that we quantify here for phishing. Singh and Meenu~\citep{singh2020phishing} survey recent machine-learning approaches to phishing-URL detection and document that the standard evaluation protocol in the recent literature remains the random train/test split, with no temporal separation between training and evaluation data, exactly the methodological gap this paper addresses. Somesha et al.~\citep{somesha2020phishing} apply CNN and LSTM architectures to URL-based phishing detection and find that deep learning does not consistently outperform Random Forest when both use comparable lexical features, a result consistent with our own finding that Random Forest is the best-performing model across all four temporal designs. Hannousse and Yahiouche~\citep{hannousse2021benchmark} construct and evaluate benchmark datasets for ML-based phishing detection and show that dataset composition, particularly the choice of negative class, systematically affects reported metrics, a finding directly aligned with our observation that the Tranco-derived legitimate class inflates the detection ceiling by 3.3 to 4.1 F1 points (Results, Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).


\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Data}
Phishing URLs were drawn from PhishTank's public feed, which is the only phishing source we found that ships a reliable, unauthenticated \texttt{submission\_time} timestamp per URL (OpenPhish's community feed and the static UCI Phishing Websites / PhiUSIIL datasets do not carry a comparable per-record date and were excluded from this design for that reason). We retained the most recent 180 days (29{,}299 URLs), the earliest weeks of the full historical feed having too few reports per week to support balanced temporal blocks. Legitimate URLs were sampled from the Tranco top-1M list~\citep{lepochat2019tranco}, chosen over alternatives such as the (now-discontinued) Alexa ranking because Tranco is specifically designed to resist the list-manipulation attacks that make raw popularity rankings an unreliable ground truth for security research. Because Tranco is a single ranked snapshot rather than a dated feed, each sampled legitimate URL was assigned a synthetic timestamp drawn uniformly over the same 180-day window as the phishing data, a deliberate simplification justified because domain reputation for an established, highly ranked site does not meaningfully change on a 180-day horizon, in contrast to phishing infrastructure, which is exactly what this study measures as changing. The final dataset is balanced by construction (29{,}299 phishing, 29{,}299 legitimate).

\subsection{Features}
We extract 32 lexical features per URL requiring no live network access (URL/hostname/path length, counts of dots/hyphens/digits/special characters, subdomain count, IP-literal hostname flag, presence of a known URL shortener, Shannon entropy of the URL and hostname strings, a count of phishing-associated keywords, and related structural features). \subsection{Temporal evaluation designs}
Data were sorted by timestamp and divided into six equal-sized blocks by sample count. We evaluate four designs: (i) \emph{random}, an 80/20 random split ignoring time, as a baseline; (ii) \emph{fixed}, training once on block 1 and evaluating separately on blocks 2 through 6, to isolate pure degradation with distance; (iii) \emph{sliding window}, training on block $k$ and evaluating on block $k+1$ for each consecutive pair, simulating periodic retraining on only the most recent data; (iv) \emph{cumulative}, training on blocks $1$ through $k$ and evaluating on block $k+1$, simulating retraining that keeps the full history. All models (random forest~\citep{breiman2001random}, XGBoost~\citep{chen2016xgboost}, logistic regression) share identical standardized features across designs.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
We regress F1 on temporal distance for the fixed design, per model, pooled naively across models, and pooled with a model fixed effect (ANCOVA: \texttt{f1 \textasciitilde{} distance + model}) to separate the within-model trend with distance from the large between-model differences in baseline F1, which a naive pooled regression otherwise folds into its residual variance and thereby understates the trend's significance. Because a linear regression with only three models provides limited power to detect a common monotonic trend regardless of specification, we additionally run a Page test for a monotonic decreasing trend, treating the three models as judges ranking the five temporal-distance conditions, and cross-check its normal-approximation $p$-value with an exact Monte Carlo permutation null (100,000 iterations) given the test's low nominal power at only three judges. We compare the sliding-window and cumulative strategies with a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test per model.

\section{Results}

A per-model OLS regression of F1 on temporal distance is directionally negative for all three models but reaches significance only for the pooled fit ($\beta = -0.00136$, $p = 0.022$, $R^2=0.34$); individually, logistic regression is marginal ($p=0.062$) and the two tree ensembles are not significant on their own ($p=0.25$ and $p=0.19$). This naive pooled fit, however, conflates two different sources of variance: the large, fixed differences in baseline F1 between models (logistic regression trails the two tree ensembles by roughly a full percentage point throughout) and the within-model trend with distance that is the actual quantity of interest. Controlling for model as a fixed effect (ANCOVA) removes the former from the residual: the slope estimate is unchanged ($\beta=-0.00136$, since the design is balanced across models) but its precision improves substantially ($p=0.0048$, $R^2=0.69$), because the between-model noise that was inflating the naive test's residual variance is no longer attributed to the distance trend. We treat the Page test as a further, specification-free check of the same conclusion: treating the three models as judges ranking F1 across the five temporal-distance conditions, the test rejects the null of no consistent ordering ($L=112$, $Z=-2.66$, $p=0.004$, one-sided). While there is a visible non-monotonic spike at distance 4 (block 5) for all three models (Figure~\ref{fig:degradacion}), the Page test is sensitive to overall ordinal trend rather than requiring strict monotonicity at every step, making it an appropriate contrast here. With only three judges, the test's normal approximation is known to have limited power and reliability, so we additionally computed an exact Monte Carlo permutation null (100,000 iterations, independently permuting each model's ranking across the five conditions under the null of no association between rank and temporal distance): the permutation-based $p$-value ($p=0.0026$, one-sided) agrees closely with, and is if anything slightly more extreme than, the asymptotic estimate, so the small-sample caveat does not change the conclusion. All three approaches (naive pooled regression, model-controlled ANCOVA, and the non-parametric Page test, now itself cross-checked by exact permutation) agree on a real, if small, degradation trend common to all three models, with the ANCOVA giving the most statistically defensible estimate of its significance.

Comparing mitigation strategies, cumulative retraining outperforms sliding-window retraining for every model (Random Forest: 0.9952 vs.\ 0.9950; XGBoost: 0.9945 vs.\ 0.9939; Logistic Regression: 0.9931 vs.\ 0.9923) (Figure~\ref{fig:estrategias}); a size-matched control (Limitations) shows this advantage is mostly, and for logistic regression entirely, a consequence of cumulative retraining's larger training-set size rather than of retaining historical examples being helpful per se, so we do not read this comparison as evidence that history itself is broadly beneficial. The paired Wilcoxon test on the five block-level F1 values per model has too few pairs to reach conventional significance regardless of the true effect size ($p=0.125$--$0.25$), so we instead compare the two strategies with a paired bootstrap over the underlying predictions (2{,}000 resamples per evaluation block, pooled across the five blocks): this confirms cumulative retraining outperforms sliding-window retraining for all three models, with 95\% confidence intervals excluding zero (Random Forest: $+0.00024$, 95\% CI $[0.00011, 0.00040]$; XGBoost: $+0.00067$, 95\% CI $[0.00035, 0.00099]$; Logistic Regression: $+0.00080$, 95\% CI $[0.00051, 0.00109]$; all $p<0.001$). The effect is real but small in absolute F1 terms, consistent with the modest overall degradation reported above.

\section{Discussion}

Degradation from temporal displacement is real and statistically supported in aggregate, but far smaller in magnitude than the field's framing of concept drift as a first-order threat would suggest, an unglamorous result but the one the data support. We think this is substantially explained by the design of the negative class rather than by the phishing detection task being inherently easy: our legitimate URLs come from Tranco's top-ranked, long-established domains, whose lexical signature (short, clean hostnames, no IP literals, low entropy, absence of phishing-associated keywords) is so different from phishing URLs that almost any reasonable classifier separates the two classes with ease, and that separation does not depend much on which specific phishing campaigns happen to be in the training set. A harder, more realistic negative class, such as newly registered legitimate domains~\citep{agarwal2026phishing} or domains with URL structures that superficially resemble phishing (long paths, subdomains, marketing redirect chains), would very plausibly show a larger temporal gap, because the decision boundary would then depend more on the specific lexical tricks currently in fashion among phishers rather than on the broad, temporally stable distinction between an established domain and an ad hoc one. Recent large-scale evidence on newly registered domains confirms this intuition: Agarwal and Vasek~\citep{agarwal2026phishing} show that phishing campaigns overwhelmingly exploit newly registered domains that have not yet accumulated the lexical simplicity that top-ranked Tranco sites carry by definition, a finding that implies any evaluation constrained to Tranco-class negatives will underestimate the operational difficulty of the classification task.

Direct feature-level evidence supports this reading. The distributional shift in individual lexical features between the earliest and latest temporal blocks is modest (Figure~\ref{fig:desplazamiento}): \texttt{url\_entropy} shows a small rightward shift, but \texttt{url\_length} is essentially unchanged and both \texttt{num\_suspicious\_keywords} and \texttt{has\_ip\_address} remain near-constant, low-variance flags throughout the whole period. Feature importance is correspondingly stable across all six temporal blocks (Figure~\ref{fig:importancia}): \texttt{is\_https}, \texttt{path\_length}, \texttt{num\_slashes}, and \texttt{url\_length} dominate in every block, with no block introducing a qualitatively different top feature. The model is not relying on a fragile, campaign-specific signal that erodes over time; it is relying on a small set of structural URL properties that stay informative because the negative class's lexical signature stays put.

Because these four features are also plausible artifacts of how the legitimate class was constructed (Tranco supplies bare domains, which are structurally shorter and path-free compared to full phishing URLs), we directly tested whether they are necessary for the near-ceiling performance by re-running the fixed-training design (block 1 versus blocks 2--6) with all four removed, leaving 28 features. Table~\ref{tab:ablation} shows the result: F1 drops by 3.3 to 4.1 percentage points across the three models (e.g., random forest: 0.9943 to 0.9617 on average across displaced blocks), confirming that these four features carry real, non-trivial separating power and that part of the near-ceiling result reflects URL-construction differences between the two classes rather than phishing-specific content alone. At the same time, F1 with those four features removed remains at 0.95--0.96, far above chance and only modestly below the full-feature result, so the remaining 28 lexical features carry substantial phishing-discriminating signal on their own. We read this as a partial, not a full, explanation of the near-ceiling result: the four structural features inflate the reported ceiling by a few points, but they are not the sole reason detection is easy.

The operational risk, then, is not that lexical phishing detectors trained today will fail outright next month. It is that the near-perfect performance reported under a random split (or, as we show, even under most temporal designs against an easy negative class) is a poor guide to performance against near-miss legitimate traffic, which is the regime that actually matters operationally. We would expect the degradation trend that is significant here in aggregate to become the dominant effect, not a marginal one, once the negative class is made realistically hard.

\section{Limitations}

Our features are purely lexical; we do not use page content, WHOIS data, or network/hosting features, all of which could shift the balance between temporal drift and raw separability. PhishTank's reports carry a known reporting-time bias (submission time is not necessarily attack-launch time, and popular/obvious phishing campaigns are reported faster than sophisticated ones). The 180-day window, while the longest span with consistently high weekly volume in the feed we used, is short relative to multi-year drift dynamics reported elsewhere in the literature. Most importantly, as discussed above, the legitimate-URL sampling from top-ranked Tranco domains very likely understates the real-world temporal degradation a deployed system would face against a harder, more realistic negative class; we consider this the most consequential limitation of the design and the most promising direction for a follow-up study. One further point bears directly on this concern: legitimate URLs receive a synthetic timestamp drawn uniformly over the 180-day window, which makes roughly half of each temporal block i.i.d. with respect to the legitimate class by construction; the small measured degradation could partly reflect this design choice rather than a property of real-world phishing detection. Two of our four designs also confound training-set size with the variable of interest. The random-split baseline in Table~\ref{tab:degradation} trains on 80\% of the full dataset, versus roughly one-sixth (block 1 alone) for the fixed-training design, so part of the random-vs.-temporal gap could reflect the difference in training-set size rather than temporal distance alone; our main degradation-trend analysis relies on the fixed design's within-design trend across distance (constant training set, so this specific confound does not apply there) and on the model-controlled ANCOVA and Page test, not on the random-vs.-fixed comparison itself, but Table~\ref{tab:degradation} is still presented as a headline number and should be read with this caveat. Similarly, cumulative retraining's training set grows with $k$ while sliding-window's stays fixed at one block, so cumulative's small but significant F1 advantage (Results) is also consistent with it simply having more training data at later blocks, rather than simply with retaining older examples being helpful per se. We ran the size-matched control directly: for each evaluation block, training on a stratified random subsample of the cumulative history downsampled to exactly one block's size (10 repetitions per block, averaged), holding training volume fixed at the sliding-window level while varying only which examples (recent-only vs.\ a random historical mix) are included. The result is model-dependent rather than uniform. For random forest, the size-matched F1 (0.9949) is statistically indistinguishable from sliding-window's (0.9950), so its cumulative advantage (0.9952) is almost entirely a volume effect. For XGBoost, the size-matched F1 (0.9942) sits roughly halfway between sliding-window (0.9939) and full cumulative (0.9945), splitting the advantage about evenly between volume and a genuine effect of retaining older examples. For logistic regression, the size-matched F1 (0.9916) is \emph{lower} than sliding-window's (0.9923): a random mix of historical examples at fixed volume actively hurts this model relative to training on the most recent block alone, so its full-cumulative advantage (0.9931) comes entirely from added volume, and then some, not from favorable example selection. Retaining historical data is therefore not uniformly beneficial once volume is controlled for; whether it helps, is neutral, or mildly hurts depends on the model family.
\section{Conclusion}

Evaluating three lexical phishing classifiers under four temporal designs on 180 days of PhishTank data, we find a small but statistically supported degradation trend when moving from a random split to a temporally displaced evaluation (Page test $p=0.004$), and that cumulative retraining consistently, though not always significantly, outperforms sliding-window retraining, an advantage a size-matched control traces mostly to training-set volume rather than to retaining historical examples per se, with the split between the two explanations itself depending on the model. The magnitude of the effect is modest, well under one F1 point in most configurations, which we attribute chiefly to a negative class (long-established top domains) that is lexically easy to separate from phishing regardless of when the phishing examples were collected, alongside the fact that assigning synthetic timestamps to the legitimate URLs makes half of each evaluation block i.i.d. by construction, halving the observable degradation effect before any evaluation even begins. We recommend that future phishing-detection evaluations pair temporal splitting with a harder negative class before concluding that concept drift is a marginal concern for this task.
